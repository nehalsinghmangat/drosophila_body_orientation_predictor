All three reviewers converge on two structural problems: (A) train/test
leakage from a row-level random split of overlapping time-delay windows,
and (B) circularity from using thrust to both correct the target heading
and predict it. These are verified as real in the current code (utils.py,
notebooks/model_training.ipynb), not just reviewer speculation, so they
are treated as non-negotiable (Essential) below. Everything else is
ordered by how much it blocks re-review.

trajectories, 101,576 frames, median MAAE 8.34/10.51 unaug/aug) do not
match the numbers the reviewers quote (2,340 trajectories, 21,129/64,026
frames, ~5 deg). Reconcile which manuscript version was reviewed before

  CAVEAT (see ROTATION AUGMENTATION and FOLLOW-UP, WINDOW-SIZE SWEEP
  sections below for the full results): the "no rotation augmentation"
  and "no time-delay embedding" rows above look competitive with, or
  better than, the full model partly because they are evaluated on the
  single real wind direction present in this dataset AND were both
  trained without rotation augmentation, which collapses their accuracy
  (to 14-28 deg mean error) the moment they are evaluated on a wind
  direction they were not trained on. Once retrained WITH augmentation
  (part5 notebook), removing the time-delay window turns out to have
  almost no cost at all (window=1 with augmentation: 5.33/6.35 deg,
  matching window=4's 5.55/6.39 deg) — so "no time-delay embedding" is a
  genuinely defensible simplification once augmentation is present, it
  was just not tested that way in Part 1. "No rotation augmentation"
  remains the one ablation that is NOT defensible under any framing —
  augmentation is required for the model to generalize across wind
  directions at all.

  - Thrust ablation costs nothing (in fact scores marginally better):
    directly answers the circularity concern raised by Reviewer #1 and #3
    — accuracy does not depend on having thrust as an input.
  - The ANN now clearly beats every regression/direction baseline by a
    real margin (the broken-data run did not show this — this is the
    first analysis that actually justifies using a neural network here).
  - Why is the full-feature ANN (5.55 deg) worse than the thrust-ablated
    one (4.95 deg), given both are trained identically (grouped split,
    grouped CV, WITH rotation augmentation) and differ only in whether
    thrust/thrust_angle are inputs? Evidence points to mild overfitting
    rather than a fluke: the full model's OWN grouped grid-search CV
    score was BETTER than the thrust-ablated model's CV score (-0.059 vs
    -0.066, higher is better), yet it is WORSE on the true held-out test
    set — a reversal that is a classic overfitting signature. Thrust is a
    second-derivative-based, differentiation-noisy feature with
    near-zero raw linear correlation to heading (OLS linear-thrust-alone
    R^2 = 0.000) and is also the exact feature the label-construction
    procedure uses to break ties — a plausible combination of "noisy
    feature" and "residual label-construction shortcut" that a
    fixed-capacity, fixed-epoch-budget network can latch onto during
    training without it paying off on genuinely held-out trajectories.
    Effect size is modest (~0.6 deg) and CIs are close to touching, so
    report this as a real, direction-consistent, modest effect — not a
    dramatic one.
  - IMPORTANT CORRECTION, added after checking rotation generalization
    (part4 notebook, see below): the "ANN, no rotation augmentation"
    variant's apparent edge over the full model (4.95 deg vs 5.55 deg) is
    NOT a third instance of "simpler is better" alongside thrust-ablation
    — it is overfitting to the single real wind direction in this
    dataset, and should not be grouped with the thrust-ablation finding.
    See the ROTATION AUGMENTATION section below.

DOWNWIND/UPWIND/CROSSWIND — the key finding, now fully resolved:

  The manuscript's exact wording ("flies were headed downwind") is
  ambiguous between two conventions: true body heading vs. direction of
  travel (groundspeed direction). Both were computed, for all 24 models/
  baselines evaluated (see part3 notebook and
  pipelinedata/reviewer_response/directional_breakdown_by_{heading,travel_direction}.csv).
  Under EITHER convention, for EVERY model tested, downwind is the
  EASIEST regime, not the hardest — directly contradicting the
  manuscript's claim:

      Model                          Upwind        Crosswind     Downwind
      ANN (full features)         5.8/8.1 deg    11.8/8.7 deg   5.1/4.4 deg
      ANN (thrust-ablated)        5.4/7.4 deg    10.4/8.2 deg   4.6/4.0 deg
      OLS (linear): ground       47.5/34.5 deg   28.6/15.3 deg  4.4/3.8 deg
      OLS (circular): ground    112.5/75.7 deg   30.1/26.5 deg  5.2/3.7 deg
      OLS (linear): thrust      169.7/115.4 deg  63.1/41.8 deg  8.9/7.0 deg
      Baseline: thrust direction 33.2/28.7 deg   38.4/22.4 deg 12.5/11.0 deg
      (heading-convention / travel-direction-convention)

  Mechanistic explanation: wind blows toward -x (CLAUDE.md convention).
  A fly flying upwind is fighting the wind, so its groundspeed can shrink
  toward zero as thrust nearly cancels the headwind — and a near-zero
  vector's ANGLE is numerically unstable (dividing near-zero components),
  which is exactly why groundspeed/thrust-dependent baselines blow up to
  100+ degrees specifically upwind. Downwind, groundspeed and airspeed
  reinforce rather than cancel, so direction is well-determined. This is
  a real, mechanistically-explicable, dataset-wide effect — not noise,
  not a data-quality artifact of the previously-broken pipeline, and not
  resolved by picking one classification convention over the other.
  The ANN is comparatively robust to this (it never blows up to 100+
  degrees the way the simple baselines do) but is still measurably worse
  upwind/crosswind than downwind. This needs to be addressed directly in
  the response letter and probably in the manuscript text — either the
  downwind claim needs to be corrected to an upwind claim, or there is a
  difference in what the original authors meant by "downwind" that has
  not been identified from the repository alone.

ROTATION AUGMENTATION — does it actually help generalization? (follow-up
check, notebooks/model_training_based_on_reviewer_feedback_part4_rotation_generalization.ipynb):

  The Part 1 finding that "ANN, no rotation augmentation" (4.95 deg) beats
  the full model (5.55 deg) is measured entirely on the held-out test set,
  which is drawn from the SAME real wind-tunnel data as training — i.e.
  the same single, fixed lab-frame wind direction throughout. Rotation
  augmentation's whole purpose is to trade a little in-distribution
  accuracy for the ability to generalize to a DIFFERENT wind direction —
  a same-distribution test set cannot see that trade-off by construction.
  This was checked directly: the held-out test trajectories were rotated
  to 8 evenly-spaced wind directions never seen in training (0/45/.../315
  degrees), and model_full, model_no_aug, and model_abl (thrust-ablated,
  also trained WITH augmentation) were each evaluated at every rotation.

      Model                                    At 0 deg   Mean +/- std across all 8 rotations
      ANN, full (trained WITH augmentation)     5.55 deg   6.39 +/- 0.44 deg
      ANN, thrust-ablated (WITH augmentation)   4.95 deg   6.00 +/- 0.66 deg
      ANN, NO rotation augmentation             4.95 deg   14.11 +/- 4.74 deg
      ANN, no time-delay embedding              4.96 deg   27.59 +/- 10.93 deg (peaks at 37.5 deg)

  Rotation augmentation is doing exactly what it is supposed to: the two
  augmented models stay in a tight band across all 8 unseen rotations
  (std under 0.7 deg), while the non-augmented models — despite winning
  at the one real wind direction — degrade sharply the moment the wind
  direction changes at all. This second check was necessary because
  `model_no_embed`'s own training set was ALSO built with
  `wind_augment=False` (same confound as `model_no_aug`, even though its
  ablation is nominally about time-delay embedding, not augmentation) —
  and it turns out to be the LEAST rotation-robust of all four models,
  worse even than "no augmentation" (std 10.93 deg vs 4.74 deg).
  CONCLUSION (as first stated): neither "ANN, no rotation augmentation"
  NOR "ANN, no time-delay embedding" should be reported as evidence that
  those features/mechanisms are unnecessary, since neither was evaluated
  under any wind direction other than the one training used. Only the
  thrust-ablation finding survives this correction (both the full and
  thrust-ablated models were trained WITH augmentation and are both
  comparably rotation-robust: std 0.44 vs 0.66 deg) — "thrust costs
  nothing" still stands as a clean, unconfounded simplification result.
  Full table: pipelinedata/reviewer_response/rotation_generalization_check.csv.

      10 (60 inputs) 5.72 deg     7.30 +/- 0.99 deg   <- worst on both metrics

  Once augmentation is present, window size barely matters: window=1 (a
  single current frame, 6 inputs) is statistically indistinguishable from
  window=4 on both real-direction accuracy and rotation-robustness, and
  window=3 is marginally the best choice tested on both metrics
  simultaneously. Larger windows (8, 10) are mildly WORSE on both metrics,
  consistent with extra lagged, noisy, redundant features adding more
  noise than signal (the same story already used to explain the
  thrust-ablation result). Full table:
  pipelinedata/reviewer_response/window_size_sweep.csv.

  NOTE (window=1 IS "no time-delay embedding", not merely similar to it):
  `collect_offset_rows` at w=1 produces exactly one column per input
  variable, an unshifted copy of the current frame - there is no lag, no
  history. "ANN, no time-delay embedding" (Part 1) and "window=1" (this
  sweep) are the identical model; the finding above is the literal claim
  that dropping the time-delay embedding entirely costs nothing, not an
  approximation of it.

  MULTI-SEED FOLLOW-UP (is window=3's single-seed edge real?) —
  notebooks/model_training_based_on_reviewer_feedback_part6_window_multiseed.ipynb:
  windows {1, 3, 4} were each retrained at 4 seeds (42, 7, 13, 99, the
  same seeds used throughout this response) to check whether window=3's
  apparent edge survives seed variability, given that Part 1's own
  multi-seed check on window=4 already showed +/-0.21 deg spread from
  initialization alone - comparable to the window=3-vs-others gap.

      Window   Real-direction (mean +/- std, 4 seeds)   Rotation-mean (mean +/- std, 4 seeds)
      1              5.16 +/- 0.37 deg                       6.50 +/- 0.24 deg
      3              5.88 +/- 1.31 deg                       7.20 +/- 1.92 deg
      4 (paper's)    5.35 +/- 0.22 deg                       6.33 +/- 0.04 deg

  RESULT: window=3's single-seed advantage in the sweep was noise, not a
  real effect - one of its 4 seeds (seed=13) landed at 7.80/10.08 deg, a
  clear outlier versus its other three seeds (4.89-5.57/6.14-6.29 deg),
  and that one bad run is what drags window=3's cross-seed mean and std
  above both window=1 and window=4. Windows 1 and 4 are both tight and
  stable across all 4 seeds and their ranges overlap almost completely.
  FINAL CONCLUSION: window size does not matter between 1 and 4. Time-
  delay embedding does NOT independently contribute meaningful accuracy
  or rotation-robustness once augmentation is controlled for - a single
  current frame of kinematics carries essentially all the recoverable
  signal, and this is now confirmed across seeds, not just a single
  favorable run. The manuscript's window=4 choice is a reasonable, stable
  choice (not measurably worse than the alternatives) but not uniquely
  necessary - window=1 would be an equally defensible, far simpler
  choice, and this is worth stating plainly rather than implying window=4
  was tuned for a reason the data supports. Full table:
  pipelinedata/reviewer_response/window_size_multiseed_check.csv.

DO THE TWO "HANDLE DYNAMIC MOMENTS" MECHANISMS ACTUALLY HELP DURING DYNAMIC
MOMENTS? (follow-up, notebooks/model_training_based_on_reviewer_feedback_part7_dynamic_moments.ipynb):

  Both `convex_opt_heading_correction` and time-delay embedding are motivated by
  the same stated rationale: help specifically during rapid turns/saccades/
  high-force maneuvers. Every prior analysis measured these two mechanisms
  averaged over a whole test set; this notebook stratifies by a wrap-safe
  per-frame turning-rate metric (circular_distance of consecutive final-target
  headings) to test the mechanisms specifically where they claim to matter.

  TIME-DELAY EMBEDDING: no benefit at any dynamism level, including exactly
  where it was supposed to help. Error rises with turning rate for both models
  (as expected -- dynamic moments are genuinely harder), but window=4 vs.
  window=1 stay within 0.04-0.34 deg of each other in every quartile:

      Turning-rate quartile   window=4   window=1
      Q1 (calmest)             4.79 deg   4.64 deg
      Q2                       5.08 deg   4.76 deg
      Q3                       5.82 deg   6.12 deg
      Q4 (most dynamic)        8.82 deg   9.05 deg
      Saccade-like (top decile of turning rate)  10.56 deg   10.60 deg

  This closes the obvious follow-up to Parts 5-6's "window size doesn't matter
  on average" finding, and the answer is no benefit here either: there is no
  dynamism regime in which time-delay embedding earns its stated purpose.

  CONVEX-OPT CORRECTION: a small, real, but inconsistent benefit -- two
  stratifications of the same mechanism disagree with each other.

      By turning rate (naive -> convex, deg):     By thrust magnitude (naive -> convex, deg):
      Q1 (calmest/lowest)   1.28 -> 1.09 (-0.19)   Q1 (lowest thrust)   2.58 -> 1.78 (-0.80)
      Q2                    1.42 -> 1.24 (-0.18)   Q2                   2.16 -> 1.70 (-0.46)
      Q3                    1.85 -> 1.63 (-0.22)   Q3                   1.75 -> 1.64 (-0.11)
      Q4 (most dynamic/highest) 3.70 -> 3.24 (-0.46)  Q4 (highest thrust)  1.29 -> 1.27 (-0.02)

  By turning rate, the benefit grows with dynamism (-0.19 to -0.46 deg) -- modest
  genuine support for the manuscript's rationale. By thrust magnitude -- the
  literal variable the correction's weighting term uses -- the pattern reverses:
  the benefit is largest at LOW thrust and nearly vanishes at HIGH thrust, the
  opposite of "body heading is most reliably constrained by thrust direction
  during high-force... maneuvers." (Caveat: the optimization weights by
  within-trajectory-normalized thrust, not pooled absolute thrust, so this is not
  a perfectly precise probe of the algorithm's internal logic, but it does show
  no high-thrust-specific benefit when thrust is measured directly.)

  ALPHA2 SENSITIVITY, DYNAMIC VS. CALM: even in the most dynamic quartile,
  alpha2 sensitivity remains negligible (3.24-3.27 deg across alpha2 in
  {1,2,5,10,20}), no more sensitive than the calm quartile (1.091-1.093 deg).
  The thrust-alignment term is not doing measurably different work as alpha2
  varies, even where the design rationale says it should matter most.

  BOTTOM LINE: time-delay embedding does not earn its stated purpose at any
  dynamism level and should not be defended on "it helps during saccades"
  grounds. Convex-opt correction earns a small, real, but partial and
  direction-inconsistent benefit -- real by turning rate, absent-to-reversed by
  thrust magnitude, and insensitive to alpha2 even in dynamic frames. Full
  tables: pipelinedata/reviewer_response/time_delay_by_dynamism.csv,
  convex_opt_by_dynamism.csv, alpha2_sensitivity_by_dynamism.csv.

VISUAL CHECK: RAW VS. NAIVE VS. NAIVE+CONVEX-OPT, FOR DYNAMIC TRAJECTORIES
WHERE THE CORRECTIONS ACTUALLY DISAGREE (follow-up, notebooks/model_training_based_on_reviewer_feedback_part8_dynamic_correction_visual_check.ipynb):

  Ground truth body orientation was never measured directly in this dataset (only
  the camera-derived ellipse angle, ambiguous modulo pi) - this is why the
  naive/convex-opt pipeline exists, and why Part 7's numerical comparison against
  "the final target" can only measure self-consistency with the pipeline's own
  later smoothing stage, not true accuracy. This notebook is the direct visual
  complement: a 3-panel plot (no correction / after naive / after naive+convex-opt)
  for every one of the 38 held-out test trajectories that are BOTH dynamic
  (contain a saccade-like turning rate, >=5.72 deg/frame) AND show a real
  naive-vs-convex disagreement (>90 deg somewhere in the trajectory) - all 38
  generated and embedded, not a sample, with the exact disagreeing frame(s) marked
  with a black star on all three panels (a plain visual comparison without this
  marker made single-frame disagreements very hard to spot by eye, even in
  cases confirmed by the data to have a full 180-degree flip).

  Direct inspection of 5 representative cases (spanning the full severity range)
  found:
  - One clear, convincing win for convex-opt (`20130401_180544_10304`): naive-only
    correction has one obviously spurious heading arrow pointing backward relative
    to an otherwise smooth trail; convex-opt removes it entirely.
  - Several cases where the disagreement is not an isolated blip but spans 29-49
    consecutive frames concentrated at a single sharp turning point - naive and
    convex both produce a smooth, plausible-looking trail through the turn, they
    just disagree about which end is the head for the whole turn, and neither
    looks obviously wrong by eye. This is not resolvable by visual inspection
    alone - it is exactly the ground-truth gap that motivated using the
    convex-opt correction in the first place.

  BOTTOM LINE: convex-opt correction is doing real, visible work in at least some
  cases (not obviously wrong in any case inspected), but a full accounting needs a
  human to page through all 38 figures - not just this sample of 5, and not just
  Part 7's pooled numeric summary. Figures:
  figures/reviewer_response_part8_dynamic_correction_comparison/*.{svg,png} (38 of
  each). Trajectory list with severity metrics:
  pipelinedata/reviewer_response/dynamic_correction_comparison_trajectories.csv.

WHY ARE 30% OF TRAJECTORIES REJECTED — IS CONVEX-OPT RUINING THEM, OR IS THE
FILTER TOO STRICT? (follow-up, notebooks/model_training_based_on_reviewer_feedback_part9_rejection_cause.ipynb):
THIS IS A MAJOR FINDING, likely the single most consequential result in this
whole response.

  data_pipeline.ipynb rejects a trajectory if its residual frame-to-frame jump on
  the NAIVE+CONVEX-OPT corrected heading reaches pi/2 anywhere. 1,001 of 3,339
  candidates (30%) are rejected this way. Using only pipelinedata/02_augmented
  and the ALREADY-COMPUTED pipelinedata/03_corrected (no new MOSEK solves
  needed), every candidate's naive-only jump was compared to its already-computed
  naive+convex-opt jump:

      996 of 1,001 rejections (99.5%): fine at naive-only (jump < 90 deg),
                                        REJECTED BECAUSE OF convex-opt.
        5 of 1,001 rejections (0.5%):  already failing at naive-only; convex-opt
                                        did not fix them.
        0 of 1,001 rejections:         failing at naive-only and RESCUED by
                                        convex-opt.

  Naive-only correction ALONE would retain 3,334 of 3,339 candidates (99.85%). It
  is specifically the convex-opt step that drives retention down to 70%.

  The 996 introduced jumps are not marginal: median 173.8 deg (60% exceed 170
  deg, i.e. near-complete pi flips, not small nudges over the 90 deg line), and
  78% occur at BELOW-average thrust for that trajectory (median ratio 0.60) -
  the opposite of what the thrust-alignment term's design rationale predicts for
  a genuine high-force correction event.

  Spatial trajectory plots (the same style used in Part 8) again proved too
  subtle to judge a pure heading flip by eye - a flipped trail looks nearly
  identical to the original since only "which end is forward" changes. A time-
  series plot of unwrapped heading vs. frame index removed the ambiguity
  completely and showed, across 4 examples spanning the full range from
  smallest to largest introduced jump and the single longest trajectory (833
  frames):
    - Convex-opt disagreeing with an otherwise flat, stable naive-only signal
      for an isolated stretch of frames, then flipping back - no physical basis
      in the naive-only signal, which already showed no real turning there.
    - Instability concentrated at trajectory BOUNDARIES - consistent with the
      current production convex_opt_heading_correction no longer anchoring k=0
      at trajectory endpoints (the pre-merge, buggy local version of this
      function used to have that anchor specifically to prevent this).
    - In the longest trajectory tested, convex-opt disagreeing with naive-only
      across nearly the ENTIRE trajectory from frame zero (not a late localized
      error), then becoming internally unstable with a second jump partway
      through.
  None of the 4 examples looked like a real saccade naive-only missed; all 4
  looked like the optimizer making an unforced, unjustified choice.

  BOTTOM LINE: the pi/2 filter is not too strict, and this is not primarily a
  labeling-ambiguity problem. Convex-opt correction, as currently configured
  (alpha1=1, alpha2=5, no boundary anchor), is net HARMFUL to trajectory
  retention - it is responsible for essentially all of the 30% rejection rate,
  discarding good data that a much simpler naive-only heuristic already handled
  correctly. This is a stronger and more actionable finding than the earlier
  "convex-opt gives a small, real, but partial accuracy benefit on the
  trajectories that survive it" (Part 7): the cost in discarded good data
  appears to outweigh that benefit. Reintroducing a boundary anchor and/or
  revisiting the alpha1/alpha2 balance (or normalizing thrust magnitude
  differently) are the natural next things to try, and could plausibly recover
  most of the 996 unnecessarily-discarded trajectories. Full data:
  pipelinedata/reviewer_response/rejection_cause_analysis.csv. Figures:
  figures/reviewer_response_part9_*.

DO THE OLS/CIRCULAR-REGRESSION BASELINES ALSO GENERALIZE ACROSS WIND
DIRECTIONS, OR ONLY THE ANN? (follow-up, notebooks/model_training_based_on_reviewer_feedback_part10_ols_rotation_generalization.ipynb):

  Part 1's OLS/circular baselines were fit on unaugmented data only. If they
  also collapse across unseen wind directions the way the ANN's own
  "no augmentation" ablation did (Part 4), "the ANN wins" would partly be an
  artifact of an unfair comparison (augmented ANN vs. non-augmented baselines).
  All 14 combos were refit on rotation-augmented training data (mirroring
  exactly how model_full was trained) and evaluated on the real test set plus
  the same 8 unseen rotations used in Parts 4-6:

      Model                                             Real dir.   Mean+/-std, 8 rotations
      ANN, full (augmented, Part 1/4)                    5.55 deg   6.39 +/- 0.44 deg
      OLS circular (air+ground), augmented-fit           6.47 deg   6.67 +/- 0.23 deg
      OLS circular (air+ground), unaugmented-fit         6.02 deg   9.15 +/- 2.55 deg
      OLS linear (thrust+ground), augmented-fit, picked
        by the real-direction metric alone                5.94 deg   17.70 +/- 7.01 deg (worst)

  RESULT: fitting the SAME simple circular regression (2 physical quantities,
  air + ground velocity direction only) on augmented data closes almost the
  entire wind-direction generalization gap with the ANN (6.67 vs 6.39 deg mean,
  and actually a TIGHTER std than the ANN). Rotation-robustness by itself is
  therefore NOT a valid reason a neural network is required - a closed-form
  circular regression achieves it too, given the same augmentation treatment.
  What survives as a genuine reason to prefer the ANN is a smaller, consistent
  accuracy margin (~0.5-0.9 deg, present both at the real direction and across
  rotations), not an inability of simpler models to handle arbitrary wind
  directions. This meaningfully softens Part 1's original "ANN beats the best
  OLS baseline by a real margin" framing - the margin is real but smaller and
  differently justified than it first appeared.

  SECOND, INDEPENDENT CONFIRMATION of Part 6's lesson: selecting a baseline (or
  hyperparameter) using only the real-direction test set is unsafe. The
  augmented-fit combo that looked BEST on the real-direction metric alone
  (`thrust+ground`, linear) was the WORST of all three conditions once
  evaluated under rotation. Any baseline reported in the manuscript needs to be
  checked under rotation before being called "the best simple alternative."
  Full data: pipelinedata/reviewer_response/ols_rotation_generalization.csv.

DOES THE ANN STILL BEAT THE BEST AUGMENTED-FIT LINEAR MODEL, BY FLIGHT-
DIRECTION REGIME? (follow-up, notebooks/model_training_based_on_reviewer_feedback_part11_ann_vs_ols_by_regime.ipynb):

  Part 10 found the ANN's overall margin over the augmented-fit circular
  (air+ground) regression is modest (~0.9 deg, 5.55 vs 6.47 deg pooled). This
  notebook checks whether that margin is uniform across upwind/crosswind/
  downwind (Part 3's regimes) or concentrated in specific ones.

      Regime (heading convention)   ANN       OLS (augmented-fit)   Gap
      Upwind                        5.84 deg  15.96 deg             ANN better by 10.1 deg
      Crosswind                    11.76 deg  26.01 deg             ANN better by 14.3 deg
      Downwind                      5.15 deg   4.32 deg             OLS better by 0.8 deg

  (Travel-direction convention: same pattern - ANN better by 9.0 deg upwind, 8.8
  deg crosswind; OLS better by 1.3 deg downwind.)

  RESULT: the pooled ~0.9 deg margin badly understates the real picture. Downwind
  is both the easiest regime (Part 3's degenerate-vector mechanism) AND the most
  common (~70-80% of frames), so it dominates the pooled average - and in that
  regime the two-variable linear model does as well as, or marginally better
  than, the ANN. But in the harder, minority regimes (upwind, crosswind), where
  directional cues become numerically near-degenerate, a linear/circular map of
  just two quantities cannot keep up, and the ANN's advantage is large and
  unambiguous (8-14 deg). CORRECT FRAMING FOR THE RESPONSE LETTER: not "the ANN
  barely helps" (Part 10's pooled number) nor "the ANN is dramatically better
  everywhere" - the ANN's benefit is concentrated exactly where translational
  kinematics alone are least informative, which is a coherent, defensible reason
  to use a neural network even though the pooled accuracy number alone does not
  show it. Full data:
  pipelinedata/reviewer_response/ann_vs_augmented_ols_by_regime.csv.
